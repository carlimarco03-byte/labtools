The dilution equation

Laboratory dilutions are commonly calculated using the relationship between the initial concentration, the initial volume, the final concentration and the final volume.

C₁ × V₁ = C₂ × V₂

This equation allows you to determine how much stock solution is required to prepare a desired final volume at a specific concentration.

Understanding the variables

C₁ — Stock concentration

The concentration of the starting stock solution.

V₁ — Stock volume

The volume of stock solution required for the dilution.

C₂ — Final concentration

The concentration you want to obtain in the final solution.

V₂ — Final volume

The total volume of the prepared solution.

Example calculation

Suppose you have a stock solution with a concentration of 10 M and want to prepare 500 mL of a solution at 1 M.

V₁ = (C₂ × V₂) / C₁

Substituting the values:

V₁ = (1 M × 500 mL) / 10 M = 50 mL

Therefore, you need 50 mL of the stock solution. The remaining volume is supplied by the solvent.

Result

Stock solution: 50 mL
Solvent: 450 mL
Final volume: 500 mL

Common mistakes

Mixing concentration units

Make sure C₁ and C₂ use compatible units. For example, do not combine M with µM without converting one of the values first.

Using the wrong final volume

V₂ represents the total final volume of the solution, not the volume of solvent added.

Forgetting the solvent volume

The amount of solvent is generally calculated as the final volume minus the stock volume.

Ignoring practical laboratory limitations

Very small calculated volumes may be difficult to pipette accurately. In such cases, preparing an intermediate dilution may be more appropriate.
