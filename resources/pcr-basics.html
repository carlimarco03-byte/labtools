What is PCR?

Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) is a molecular biology technique used to amplify a specific DNA sequence.


A PCR reaction uses repeated cycles of DNA denaturation, primer annealing and DNA extension to generate many copies of a target sequence.

Main PCR components

DNA Template

The DNA molecule containing the sequence that will be amplified.

Forward & Reverse Primers

Short DNA oligonucleotides that define the boundaries of the target sequence and provide starting points for DNA synthesis.

dNTPs

Deoxynucleotide triphosphates provide the building blocks required for synthesis of new DNA strands.

DNA Polymerase

The enzyme responsible for synthesizing new DNA strands during the extension step.

Reaction Buffer

Provides the chemical environment required for efficient DNA polymerase activity.

Water

Nuclease-free water is used to adjust the reaction to the desired final volume.

Typical PCR reaction setup

PCR components are generally prepared from concentrated stock solutions. The required volume of each component depends on its stock concentration, desired final concentration and total reaction volume.

C₁ × V₁ = C₂ × V₂

For example, if a primer has a stock concentration of 20 µM and the desired final concentration is 0.5 µM in a 25 µL reaction:

V₁ = (0.5 µM × 25 µL) / 20 µM
Primer volume

0.625 µL per reaction

Why use a master mix?

When multiple PCR reactions are prepared, it is often useful to prepare a master mix containing all components that are common to the reactions.


The master mix reduces repetitive pipetting, improves consistency between reactions and can reduce pipetting errors.


The template DNA is commonly added separately when different samples are being tested.

Master mix overage

When preparing a master mix for multiple reactions, the calculated volume is often increased slightly to compensate for pipetting losses and material retained in tubes or pipette tips.

For example, a 5% overage means preparing enough master mix for slightly more than the nominal number of reactions.

Total reactions = Sample reactions × (1 + overage)

Common mistakes

Incorrect stock concentration

Always verify the concentration and units of each stock solution before calculating reaction volumes.

Confusing stock and final concentration

The stock concentration is the concentration of the reagent you have, while the final concentration is the concentration required in the reaction.

Forgetting the reaction volume

All component volumes must fit within the specified total reaction volume.

Using too much template

Template input should be optimized for the specific assay and sample type. Excess template can sometimes interfere with amplification.
