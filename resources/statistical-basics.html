Statistical Basics

A practical introduction to common statistical concepts used when analysing laboratory and scientific data.

Why statistics matter in laboratory research

Laboratory experiments often produce multiple measurements for the same experimental condition. Statistics help researchers summarize these data, describe their variability and determine whether observed differences are likely to reflect real effects.


Good statistical analysis should begin with an understanding of the experimental design and the type of data being collected.

Mean, median and mode

The most common measures used to describe the central tendency of a dataset are the mean, median and mode.


Mean

The arithmetic average of all observations.

Mean = Σx / n

Median

The middle value when observations are arranged from smallest to largest.

Mode

The value that occurs most frequently in a dataset.


For example, consider the measurements:

10, 12, 11, 13, 12

The mean is 11.6, while the median is 12.

Variability and standard deviation

Two datasets can have the same mean but very different levels of variability. Standard deviation describes how widely individual observations are distributed around their mean.


Example

Dataset A:

10, 11, 10, 11, 10

Dataset B:

5, 15, 8, 14, 10

Both datasets have a mean of approximately 10.4, but Dataset B shows substantially greater variability.


A low standard deviation indicates that observations are relatively close to the mean, whereas a higher standard deviation indicates greater dispersion.

Range

The range provides a simple measure of variability by calculating the difference between the largest and smallest observations.

Range = maximum − minimum

For example:

5, 8, 10, 14, 15

Range = 15 − 5 = 10

Although easy to calculate, the range depends only on two observations and therefore provides less information than measures such as standard deviation.

Why biological replicates matter

Biological replicates represent independent experimental units and are essential for estimating biological variability.


Technical replicates, on the other hand, are repeated measurements of the same experimental material and primarily help assess measurement or technical variability.


Important distinction

Increasing the number of technical measurements does not necessarily provide the same statistical information as increasing the number of independent biological replicates.

Statistical significance

When comparing experimental groups, researchers often perform statistical tests to determine whether an observed difference is compatible with random variation under a specified statistical model.


A p-value is one component of this assessment. It describes how compatible the observed result, or a more extreme result, is with the null hypothesis under the assumptions of the statistical test.


A commonly used threshold is:

p < 0.05

However, statistical significance should not be interpreted as proof that an experimental effect is biologically important.

Statistical significance vs biological relevance

A statistically significant difference can be very small and have little practical or biological relevance. Conversely, an important biological effect may fail to reach statistical significance if the experiment has high variability or insufficient statistical power.


For this reason, scientific interpretation should consider effect size, variability, confidence intervals, sample size and experimental context rather than relying only on a p-value.

Correlation vs causation

Correlation describes an association between two variables. It does not by itself demonstrate that one variable causes changes in the other.


Example

Suppose plant growth and fertilizer concentration increase together in an experiment. A correlation between the two variables does not automatically prove that fertilizer concentration is the only factor responsible for the observed growth.


Experimental design, controlled variables and appropriate statistical models are required when investigating causal relationships.

Choosing a statistical test

The appropriate statistical method depends on the experimental design, the type of variables, the number of groups, the distribution of the data and the scientific question.


Two groups

For example, comparing a control group with a treatment group.

Multiple groups

For example, comparing several treatments or experimental conditions.

Association

For example, investigating whether two measured variables are related.


Common statistical approaches include t-tests, ANOVA, correlation analysis, regression and non-parametric tests. The choice should be based on the experimental design and assumptions of the method rather than simply on which test produces the desired result.

Good statistical practice

Plan before analysing

Define the experimental question and statistical approach before inspecting the results whenever possible.

Report variability

Present appropriate measures of variability alongside summary statistics.

Consider the design

Statistical analysis should reflect the structure of the experiment and the independence of observations.
